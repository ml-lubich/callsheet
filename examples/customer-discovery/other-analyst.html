Call notes: route-planning discovery, regional delivery prospect

Reviewed the recording of the 14 May call between the seller (Ines) and the prospect's ops lead (Hollis). My read below, written before seeing anyone else's notes.

Headline

This prospect has a real pain point but almost no hard data behind it. Every number Hollis gave was a guess, and to his credit he said so out loud every time rather than pretending to precision he didn't have. That honesty is actually a good sign for qualification — it means whatever gets measured in a pilot will be believed.

The process, as I heard it

Routes are built manually, every morning, by a single dispatcher working off a spreadsheet and the previous day's addresses. He prints paper sheets and hands them out at the gate around six. From there the day is at the mercy of whatever changes: a blocked gate code, a moved address, a driver who doesn't show. Hollis was blunt that this is "before anything goes wrong" — meaning the described process is already the best case.

Sizing the fleet

Roughly thirty drivers, though Hollis wouldn't commit past "thirty-something." Daily stop counts land in the high fifties to sixty, heavier on what he calls the northern run. I'd treat both of these as directionally right but not something to build a contract number on without an actual headcount pull.

Where I'd push back on the seller's math

Ines took Hollis's "somewhere north of two hours a week" and multiplied it straight through to an annual hours figure. That's a reasonable back-of-envelope move for a discovery call, but it compounds two soft numbers into one that sounds a lot more solid than it is. I wouldn't repeat the resulting total in a proposal without a caveat attached — Hollis himself only reacted with "sounds worse than it feels," which is not the same as confirming it.

The turnover admission

The most interesting moment for me wasn't the hours estimate, it was the turnover number. Hollis started to say twelve drivers left last year, caught himself, and landed on nine — and immediately hedged that not all of it was routing-related. That kind of self-correction, live, usually means the real number is one he's thought about before but doesn't have memorized, which tells you turnover isn't being tracked against this cause the way it probably should be.

Money

Notably absent: any dollar figure. Hollis explicitly declined to price driver-hours, twice, and pointed at payroll instead. Whoever builds the business case for this deal will need that number from someone who isn't on this call, and I'd flag that as an open dependency rather than assume it'll surface on its own.

Where this stands

No approver was named — Hollis deflected the sign-off question until "the numbers hold up," which is a fair position but also means there's no confirmed buyer yet. What he did commit to were two concrete, testable conditions: a quiet week on the northern run, and the dispatcher off the phones close to an hour earlier than usual. Both of those are things a thirty-day pilot on just that one route could plausibly produce evidence for, which is probably why the conversation converged there. My one disagreement with how the call ended: I'd have pushed harder, in the room, to at least get a name for who reviews the pilot results, rather than leaving that for a follow-up.
